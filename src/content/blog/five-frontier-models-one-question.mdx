I build a chat product that sends one question to several AI models at once and
shows the answers side by side. That makes it easy to run a small experiment I
kept wondering about. If you put the same hard question to five of the best
models available, do they actually disagree? Or are they five paraphrases of one
answer?

I ran it twice. The first time, in July, the answer was mostly paraphrases. The
second time, this week, every single question split the room. The models were
partly different between the two runs but that is not the main reason. The
questions were.

## How the test works

Each question comes with exactly two options and an instruction to pick one and
give a reason in two sentences. "It depends" is not allowed. Five models answer
in parallel, each from a different company, and a script tallies the picks.

In July the five were Claude Opus, GPT-5.6 Sol, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Llama 4 Maverick
and DeepSeek V3.1. This week they were Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4,
Llama 4 Maverick and DeepSeek V3.1. Each question was asked once per model.

## July: mostly a chorus

Eight questions, three splits. Five came back unanimous.

All five models said a solo founder should launch on Postgres rather than SQLite.
All five said to refactor a four-year-old system rather than rewrite it. All five
picked a monorepo for a five-engineer team, a free allowance over an upfront card
for an unknown product and shipping on time over slipping two weeks for one janky
feature.

None of those are bad answers. They are the answers you would find in the first
three blog posts on each topic. That is the point. The models have all read the
same blog posts.

The three that split had something in common. Whether to turn on strict
TypeScript at once or file by file went 3 to 2. Raising a seed round against
bootstrapping went 4 to 1. Whether a consultant must disclose that a model wrote
the first draft went 4 to 1. None of them is really about facts. Each turns on
how much risk you will tolerate or what you think you owe someone.

## November: questions that hide one thing

So for the second run I used questions written around that idea. Each one leaves
out the piece of information that would settle it: your appetite for risk, how
you weigh loyalty against money, what you value in a team.

Eight questions, eight splits. Five went 3 to 2 and three went 4 to 1.

- **Raising a launch price that was too low.** Grandfather early customers forever
  or move them after 12 months? Claude, GPT and Gemini said move them. Llama and
  DeepSeek said honour the old price forever.
- **Mastering one model against routing each task to the best one.** The three
  models from the big labs said master one. The two open models said route.
- **A well-liked engineer who ships slower than everyone after nine months.** Claude,
  Gemini and Llama said let them go. GPT and DeepSeek said coach for another
  quarter.
- **An agency at $30k a month where the owner is the bottleneck.** Claude and
  DeepSeek said hire full time. The other three said use contractors.
- **Annual billing at 12 months for the price of 10.** Three said not generous
  enough. Two said too generous.

The other three were closer. Four models to one said default to Server Components,
keep an AI code reviewer advisory and build retrieval over a 500 page handbook
instead of stuffing it into the context window.

## The disagreements are worth reading

A split tally is a headline. The reasons are the useful part.

On the hiring question, Claude argued that if the owner is the bottleneck,
contractors still need the owner to brief them, manage them and check their work,
so the bottleneck stays where it is. GPT picked contractors but answered that
point directly: give one contractor ownership of the whole delivery function
rather than a list of tasks, or you remain the bottleneck. Gemini added the money
case: at $30k a month one lost client can make a salary unaffordable.

On the handbook question, Gemini was the lone vote for the long context window.
It put the handbook at about 250,000 tokens, said that fits in a modern window
and said prompt caching removes most of the cost. Claude put the same handbook at
250,000 to 400,000 tokens and said sending it with every question is slow and
expensive. Roughly the same estimate of the size, opposite conclusions.

And on annual billing, DeepSeek called the discount 20 percent. Ten months for
twelve is 16.7 percent. Every other model got that right. One wrong number in
five is exactly the kind of thing you only catch when you can see the other four.

## What I take from it

Three things, with the caveats first. This is one run per model, and these
models do not give the same answer twice. The July and November questions were
different and so were some of the models. Treat it as a field note rather than a
benchmark.

First, asking several models is not useful for every question. If the answer is
in the textbook they will all recite the textbook, and the second opinion costs
time and tells you nothing.

Second, it is very useful for the questions people actually lose sleep over.
Those are the ones without a textbook answer, and they are the ones where a
single model will sound confident on one side of a line that reasonable people
draw in different places.

Third, the most valuable output was never the vote. It was the one argument
nobody else made. A single model cannot show you what it left out. Four other
models can.
